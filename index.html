The problem in two paragraphs

Most of the encryption protecting web traffic, software updates and stored records today rests on two hard problems: factoring large numbers (RSA) and finding discrete logarithms (Diffie-Hellman and elliptic curves). In 1994 Peter Shor showed that a large enough quantum computer solves both in polynomial time.

That matters before such a machine exists. An attacker can record encrypted traffic now and decrypt it later ("harvest now, decrypt later"). In August 2024 NIST published the first post-quantum standards, FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) and FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), built on lattice problems. Most people who will have to migrate have never seen either idea run. This lab lets them.

Every demo here is a toy. It teaches the mechanism with small numbers so you can watch it work. Nothing in this page protects real data.

Experiment 1. Break a lock the way Shor's algorithm does

In plain words: a lock built from a big number N = p × q is safe only if nobody can find p and q. Shor's trick turns factoring into finding how often the pattern of powers ax mod N repeats. A quantum computer finds that repeat length fast. Here a normal computer finds it by brute force, which works for tiny N and fails for real ones.

Experiment 2. Hide a secret in noise

In plain words: imagine a system of linear equations with a secret solution. Solving it is easy algebra. Now nudge every right-hand side by a small random error. Anyone holding the secret can still decrypt, because the errors stay small. Anyone solving the equations gets a wrong answer. That gap is Learning With Errors (LWE), the problem under ML-KEM. About, limits and sources

  • Toy parameters. The LWE scheme here uses n up to 32 and q = 3329. Real ML-KEM uses structured module lattices at a much larger scale, and small LWE instances can be broken with lattice reduction. The point of experiment 2 is the role of noise, not a security claim.
  • Brute force is not the best classical attack. Real RSA is attacked with the number field sieve, which is far faster than brute-force period finding. Experiment 1 shows why the quantum period-finding step matters, not the cheapest classical route.
  • No quantum simulation. The period is found classically. The page never claims a quantum speedup it did not run.
  • Sources. NIST FIPS 203 and FIPS 204 (August 2024); RFC 7748; RFC 8032; RFC 8017; Shor, "Algorithms for quantum computation: discrete logarithms and factoring" (1994); Regev, "On lattices, learning with errors, random linear codes, and cryptography" (2005).
  • Accessibility. Keyboard operable, every chart has a text table, results are announced to screen readers, and charts never use colour alone.
